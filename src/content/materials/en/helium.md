## What it is

Helium is produced by radioactive decay in the Earth's crust and accumulates in some natural gas fields. It is separated cryogenically during gas processing or liquefied natural gas production, then liquefied and shipped in insulated containers that must be delivered within weeks. Once released, it escapes the atmosphere, so it is a non-renewable resource.

## Supply chain and EU dependency

The United States and Qatar are the two largest producers, followed by Algeria and Russia, where a very large plant in the Far East has been ramping up. The EU has small production in Poland and imports the vast majority of its needs. Supply is frequently disrupted because it depends on a handful of plants: outages, the 2017 blockade of Qatar and sanctions on Russia have each caused shortages, and the sale of the United States federal helium reserve removed a historical buffer.

## Substitution and recycling

No other substance stays liquid near absolute zero, so there is no substitute in cryogenics. Argon or nitrogen can replace helium in some welding and purging applications. Recycling is closed-loop at the point of use: modern MRI scanners and large laboratories recapture and reliquefy helium, and chip fabs increasingly install recovery systems.

## What to watch

Qatari and Russian plant availability, shipping conditions through the Strait of Hormuz and the Red Sea, new helium-rich gas projects in Canada, Tanzania and South Africa, and semiconductor fab construction in Europe.
