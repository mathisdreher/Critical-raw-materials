## What it is

The heavy rare earth elements run from europium to lutetium, plus yttrium. In the EU assessment the group covers dysprosium, erbium, europium, gadolinium, holmium, lutetium, terbium, thulium, ytterbium and yttrium. They are far less abundant than the light rare earths and come mainly from ion-adsorption clays, which are leached in place, and from xenotime.

## Supply chain and EU dependency

Ion-adsorption clays are mined in southern China and, increasingly, in Myanmar, whose output is shipped to China for separation. China therefore controls essentially all separation of heavy rare earths and most of the magnet manufacturing downstream. The EU has no production and imports nearly all of its needs, largely inside finished magnets and motors rather than as oxides or metals. In April 2025 China placed seven medium and heavy rare earths, including dysprosium, terbium, samarium and yttrium, under export licensing. Shipments slowed for weeks and several European automotive suppliers interrupted production.

## Substitution and recycling

Grain-boundary diffusion has cut the dysprosium and terbium content of magnets substantially, and some motor designs avoid rare earths altogether at a cost in efficiency or size. Recycling of magnets is technically proven and is starting at industrial scale in Europe, but end-of-life recovery is still around 1%. The Critical Raw Materials Act requires large users to assess the recyclability of their magnets.

## What to watch

Chinese export licensing, separation projects in Europe, Australia, Malaysia and the United States, supply from Myanmar, and magnet recycling capacity.
