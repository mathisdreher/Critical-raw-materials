## What it is

Bismuth is one of the least toxic heavy metals, which explains its role as a substitute for lead. Almost all of it is a by-product: it is separated during the refining of lead, and to a lesser extent tungsten, tin and copper concentrates. Output therefore depends on the activity of those industries.

## Supply chain and EU dependency

China accounts for the large majority of refined bismuth, with smaller quantities from Laos, South Korea, Japan and others. The EU has limited refining, in Belgium in particular, and imports most of its consumption. In February 2025 China added bismuth to its export control list, and European prices rose several-fold in the following weeks. The bismuth market is small, so modest changes in trade policy have large price effects.

## Substitution and recycling

In pharmaceuticals and pigments bismuth is dissipated. In alloys and solders it is diluted and rarely recovered, so the end-of-life recycling rate is close to zero. Substitution is possible in most uses, but often back toward lead, which regulation is trying to remove, or toward more expensive options such as indium.

## What to watch

Chinese export licensing, EU restrictions on lead in alloys and ammunition, which raise bismuth demand, and by-product recovery at European lead and copper smelters.
