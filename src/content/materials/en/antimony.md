## What it is

Antimony is recovered mostly from the sulphide mineral stibnite and as a by-product of gold, lead and silver mining. It is traded as metal ingot and as antimony trioxide, the white powder used in flame retardants. The market is small, in the order of a hundred thousand tonnes per year, and opaque: prices are assessed by reporting agencies rather than set on an exchange.

## Supply chain and EU dependency

China has long dominated both mining and, even more, the refining of antimony, processing concentrates from Tajikistan, Russia, Myanmar and Bolivia. The EU has no mine production of note and imports ores, metal and trioxide. In 2024 China placed antimony products under export licensing and later halted shipments to some destinations, which tightened supply worldwide and multiplied prices within months. The episode exposed how little refining capacity exists outside China.

## Substitution and recycling

Most recycled antimony comes from lead-acid batteries and stays within the lead industry. Antimony used in flame retardants is dissipated and not recovered. Halogen-free flame retardants based on phosphorus or mineral fillers can substitute in many plastics, and calcium alloys have replaced antimony in part of the battery market.

## What to watch

Chinese export licensing practice, new or restarted smelters in Europe, Oman and North America, by-product output from gold mines, and regulatory pressure on halogenated flame retardant systems.
